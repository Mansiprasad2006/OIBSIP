Three scales, one physical reality

Each unit measures the same thing — how much a substance's particles are moving — starting from a different zero point.

0°C

Celsius

Zero is set at the freezing point of water and 100 at its boiling point, at sea level. Used by most of the world for weather, cooking, and everyday measurement.

32°F

Fahrenheit

Water freezes at 32 and boils at 212. Still the everyday standard in the United States, its territories, and a handful of other countries.

0 K

Kelvin

Starts at absolute zero rather than at water's freezing point, so it never goes negative. The scale scientists and engineers reach for.

The six conversions

TEMPSHIFT runs these behind the scenes every time you enter a value.

Celsius → Fahrenheit
F = (C × 9/5) + 32
Fahrenheit → Celsius
C = (F − 32) × 5/9
Celsius → Kelvin
K = C + 273.15
Kelvin → Celsius
C = K − 273.15
Fahrenheit → Kelvin
K = (F − 32) × 5/9 + 273.15
Kelvin → Fahrenheit
F = (K − 273.15) × 9/5 + 32

Where the scale ends

Absolute zero is the point at which particles hold the least possible energy. Nothing can be colder — so TEMPSHIFT won't convert a value past it.

Absolute zero is the theoretical floor of temperature itself, not just a low reading. It marks the point where atomic motion is at its minimum, and it has never been reached in a lab, only approached.

If you enter a value below this floor in any unit, TEMPSHIFT stops the conversion and explains why, instead of returning a number that can't physically exist.
